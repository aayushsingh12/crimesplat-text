“Of course, if there is a possi-
bility this girl is still alive, pull out
all of the stops,” said Beth Frerk-
ing, director of the Casey Journal-
ism Center on Children and Fami-
lies at the University of Maryland.
“But to make all of these [abduc-
tions] the same, that's when it
becomes dangerous.”

Frerking, whose center works
with journalists covering family
issues, said news organizations
tend to lump together dissimilar
cases. Combined with extensive
coverage, that can give a false
impression to adults and espe-
cially to children that abductions
by strangers are common.

Frerking had to comfort her
own 8-year-old son, who was wor-
ried about being snatched from his
bedroom after briefly seeing cov-
erage of the Elizabeth Smart case.
Frerking said she explained to him
the rarity of such situations, reas-
sured him of her watchfulness but
also told him how to react to dan-
gerous situations.

“I would tell parents with
young children to turn the TV off,”
she said.

Larry Sabato, a political and
media analyst at the University of
Virginia, supported publicizing
Jennifer's picture. But like Frerk-
ing, Sabato said media outlets
become reckless when they cause
unnecessary fear with saturation

i

coverage — whether of abduc-
tions, shark attacks, killer bees or
rare infectious diseases — as a
way to fill news holes during the
“summer doldrums.”

“No one can deny that if hav-
ing intensive coverage of the situ-
ation helps find the perpetrator,
that's a good thing,” said Sabato,
author of “Feeding Frenzy,” a book
that examines modern media cov-
erage of politics. “It's a bad thing,
however, if it gives people the false
impression that children cannot go
outside without an armed guard.”

The National Center for Miss-
ing and Exploited Children
became so concerned about this
summer’s news coverage that last
month it released a statement
urging families to remain calm.
The release explained that the
majority of missing children were
either abducted by a parent or
relative or simply ran away.

Between 3,000 and 5,000
non-family abductions are
reported annually, with 200 to 300
ending in the child being killed,
ransomed or kept, the center
reported. This year, the center
expects that figure to be closer to
100.

Peter Banks, the center's
director of training and outreach,
urged parents to talk with their
children about how to avoid being
“seduced” into going with a
stranger, which is more common.
Parents should also always know
where and with whom their chil-
dren are going. Overall, though,
Banks said the media coverage
and particularly news organiza-
tions’ embrace of the Amber Alert
are positive.

“It is an incredible role and is
sending the message . . . that we
are united as one and we are going
to use every resource we have to
find these children and bring them
safely home,” Banks said.

Cases of West Nile

Up to 10 percent of people
diagnosed with West Nile
could die from it, meaning
potentially 100 fatalities
this year, an expert said.

THE NEW YORK TIMES

The number of cases of West
Nile fever is expected to rise
sharply in the next week and could
eventually reach 1,000, federal
health officials said Thursday. If
the current epidemic reflects
experience, about 10 percent of
the cases will be fatal.

The Centers for Disease Con-
trol and Prevention said Friday
there have been 251 human cases
of the virus reported to the CDC,
including 11 deaths. Five states —
Colorado, Maryland, Massachu-
setts, Missouri, Ohio and New
York — were added this week to
the list of states with confirmed
and suspected cases in the nation’s
worst outbreak of the mosquito-
borne disease.

Dr. Lyle Petersen, an official
with the Centers for Disease Con-
trol and Prevention, said the
agency did not have detailed
information on the severity of the
patients’ illnesses. But he said a
vast majority were hospitalized
with brain inflammation from
meningitis or encephalitis.

“We can expect more cases,
and potentially a lot more cases,”
said Petersen, an epidemiologist

* who specializes in insect-borne

diseases. “We don't know where
the peak will be."
Health officials worked Friday

{

to calm public fear of the West Nile
virus after the government pre-
dicted 1,000 people could be
infected with the potentially
deadly disease this year.

The virus has been found in
every state east of the Rocky
Mountains, with human cases
confirmed in nine states and the
District of Columbia.

Petersen said the disease’s
peak may not come for several
weeks and that “a lot more cases”
could be reported before cooler
weather Kills the disease-carrying
insects.

Petersen said up to 10 percent
of people diagnosed with West
Nile could die from it — meaning
potentially 100 fatalities this year.

Beyond the sheer number of
cases, health officials are con-
cerned about the timing. In previ-
ous years, only 10 to 15 percent of
West Nile patients had symptoms
before Aug. 1; the peak time for
onset of symptoms was the last
week of August and the first week
of September. But so far this year,
Petersen said, the onset of symp-
toms in a vast majority of cases
came in July, suggesting that the
peak probably lies ahead.

For unknown reasons, the
median age of patients in this
year's epidemic is 55, about a
decade younger than in the past.
Because death rates from West
Nile tend to be higher in older
people, that may have the effect of
lowering this year's death rate.
