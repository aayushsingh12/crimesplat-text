Stuff you won’t see on ESPN

The major league baseball playoffs
are under way.

Multimillionaire athletes fill our TV
screens with acts of physical prowess.
Wealthy corporations buy commercials
that we'll see until we can recite them
from memory.

But baseball does not belong solely
to the genetically gifted or the corpo-
rate titans. It belongs to all of us, rich
or poor. In fact, it once was played
mostly by men who grew up poor. Babe
Ruth spent part of his childhood in a
school for errant boys.

Last week, Todd Meyer of Acts 2, an
inner-city Christian ministry, described
a tee ball game that took place last
summer on four lots the ministry is
using as a park and green space on a
stretch of Day Avenue in Old Southwest
Roanoke. I'd been attracted to it by the
construction of what appeared to be a
pagoda but turned out to be a picnic or
worship shelter put up by the ministry.

Perhaps 70 people were directly
involved in the game, Meyer said. Per-
haps 300 paid it some attention while at
the annual block party that was under
way.
Because they've had little or no
exposure to organized sports, kids from
ages 5 to 12 played in the neighbor-
hood’s four-team tee ball league.

During the game, one of the bigger
boys, age 11, struck a Ruthian pose and
pointed to the fence along the proper-
ty's southern border. Then, with a
mighty swat, he launched the ball high
and far.

It crossed the fence. The neighbors
roared as the batter toured the bases. It
was one of those magical moments we
see too seldom, one of those that will
never get replayed on ESPN.
